Every software project carries risk. The ones that go wrong are rarely hit by a rare disaster. They suffer from ordinary risks that nobody named, owned or watched. Good risk management is a light, regular habit that keeps those risks visible.

This guide gives technical leaders a practical method, without heavy paperwork.

## What counts as a project risk

A risk is something that might happen and would hurt the project if it did. It is different from an issue, which has already happened. Typical software risks fall into a few groups:

- **Scope risks:** unclear requirements, late changes, hidden complexity.
- **Technical risks:** unproven technology, difficult integrations, performance limits.
- **People risks:** key person dependency, turnover, skills gaps.
- **Delivery risks:** unrealistic dates, dependencies on other teams, slow decisions.
- **External risks:** vendor changes, regulation, budget cuts.

## Find the risks early

Hold a short risk session at the start, and repeat it at each major milestone. Ask the team simple questions:

- What are we least sure about?
- What would we be embarrassed to be asked about in a month?
- Which part depends on something we do not control?
- If one person left tomorrow, what would stop?

Include engineers, the product owner and a stakeholder. Different people see different risks, and the engineers usually know the technical ones first.

## Write each risk clearly

A useful risk statement has three parts: the cause, the event and the effect. For example, "Because the payment provider's sandbox differs from production, the integration may fail at launch, delaying go-live by two weeks." This is better than "payments might be a problem", because it can be tested and acted on.

## Rate and rank

Give each risk a likelihood and an impact on a simple three-point scale. Multiply them to rank the list. Do not spend time on precise numbers. The aim is to separate the handful of risks that need attention from the many that do not.

## Choose a response

For each important risk, pick one of four responses:

- **Avoid:** change the plan so the risk cannot occur, such as dropping a fragile feature.
- **Reduce:** lower the likelihood or impact, for example with an early prototype.
- **Transfer:** move the risk to someone better placed to carry it, such as using a managed service.
- **Accept:** do nothing now, but agree what would trigger action.

Prefer actions that learn something early. A two-day spike that tests the risky integration is cheaper than finding out at the end.

## Give every risk an owner and a date

A risk with no owner is a worry, not a managed item. Name one person for each high-ranked risk and set a date to review it. The owner does not have to fix it alone. They make sure it is not forgotten.

## Keep a living register

Use a simple table: description, rating, response, owner and next review. Keep it where the team already works, such as the project board. Review it for a few minutes in each planning session. Remove risks that have passed, add new ones and update the ratings.

## Watch for early signals

Define a few indicators that tell you a risk is turning into an issue. For example, rising defect counts, repeatedly reopened tickets, estimates that only increase, decisions waiting on someone for days or demos replaced by status reports. When an indicator appears, escalate while options are still cheap.

## Plan buffers honestly

Some uncertainty cannot be removed. Build explicit buffers into the schedule and budget, and be open about them. A hidden buffer gets used up silently. A visible one can be discussed and protected.

## Communicate without alarm

Share the top risks with stakeholders regularly, in plain language. Say what could happen, what you are doing about it and what you need from them. People trust teams that raise problems early far more than teams that report only good news.

## Learn from every project

At the end of a project, review which risks materialised, which did not and which were missed. Feed that into the next project's starting list. Over time, your own history becomes the best guide to what to watch.

## Summary

Name the risks, rank them, give each one an owner and keep looking. A short, regular review, a simple register and honest communication will prevent more failures than any sophisticated framework.
